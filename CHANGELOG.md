# Changelog

The book reports a field that is moving quickly, and some figures in it will date.
Corrections and updates are listed here, newest first.

## v1.2 — 2026

Corrections to medication figures, and the disclosures tightened.

- **Low-dose naltrexone: the dose range was wrong.** The book gave 0.5–6 mg per day.
  The systematic review it cites reports 1–6 mg per day, and the largest of the four
  studies in that review started at 1 mg. The lower bound had no source. Corrected in
  Chapter 3, Chapter 7 and the glossary.
- **Removed two claims that the cited review does not make.** The LDN passage credited
  "2024–2025 systematic reviews" with a finding that roughly half of patients met
  responder criteria by twelve weeks. There is one review, published in 2026, and it
  reports no such figure. Replaced with the effect sizes it does report, including its
  own rating of the evidence as low-certainty.
- **Fludrocortisone: mechanism stated more honestly, dose removed.** The book presented
  plasma-volume expansion as established and the drug as most useful in hypovolemic
  POTS. The 2019 NIH expert consensus — already cited in that chapter — states plainly
  that there is no evidence fludrocortisone increases blood volume, and none that
  hypovolemic patients respond better. The starting dose was removed rather than
  attached to a source that could not be verified.
- **Melatonin: dose, timing and the immune claim.** The dose figures had no citation at
  all. Two phase-response-curve trials now support the low-dose argument directly:
  0.5 mg and 3.0 mg produce shifts of the same size. The timing advice was also wrong
  for the stated purpose — half an hour before bed is the sleeping-pill use, while the
  trials that mapped the curve dosed in the afternoon. The immune paragraph asserted a
  direction of effect that the literature does not support; the standing review
  describes melatonin as an immune buffer and says the mechanism remains unclear.
- **Doses now carry primary sources.** Midodrine 2.5–10 mg three times daily and the
  neuropathic-versus-hyperadrenergic difference both come from one randomized
  crossover trial. The "low doses outperform high doses" claim about beta-blockers now
  cites the trial that established it. Ivabradine dosing is split across two sources,
  with a note that the upper end comes from guidance on inappropriate sinus tachycardia
  rather than POTS.
- **Disclosures.** Author and affiliation disclosure moved above the table of contents.
  Licence scope added: third-party quotations and figures are not covered by the
  Creative Commons licence. Each chapter file carries a one-line disclosure, because a
  search result can land on a chapter without passing the README.
- **Removed cohort and IRB framing** from the author's note. That observation is
  separate work with its own consent process; this book is not part of it and is not a
  route into it.
- **Layout.** The weekly diary template was not a table — it was a run of paragraphs
  with vertical bars as text, which broke in every format. It is a real table now.

## v1.1 — 2026

Bibliography overhaul. Every citation that rests on a published study now carries a
resolvable identifier.

- **Removed a reference to a work that does not exist.** The book cited "Raj SR et al.
  (2018), Current concepts in the diagnosis and treatment of POTS, *Heart* 104(16)".
  No such paper exists, and Raj SR has never published in *Heart*. Replaced with the
  2015 Heart Rhythm Society expert consensus statement, which appears to be the source
  of the distortion.
- **Corrected an attribution.** A 2024 commentary on peripheral serotonin was credited
  to Afrin LB and Molderings GJ. Its authors are Anderson GM, Cook EH, Blakely RD,
  Sutcliffe JS and Veenstra-VanderWeele J.
- **Corrected a fabricated title and study description.** The Peter RS 2025 *PLoS
  Medicine* paper was given a title that does not exist and described as a prospective
  cohort of 1,154. It is a nested case-control study of 982 cases against 576 matched
  controls.
- **Sourced a figure that had no citation.** The claim of 49 per cent higher healthcare
  utilisation and roughly thirty clinical contacts a year comes from Lin L-Y et al.
  2024, *BMC Medicine* 22(1):255 (OpenSAFELY, n=52,988).
- Corrected the locator on El-Sayed & Hainsworth 1996, the title on Bourne et al. 2021,
  and a van Campen entry that had been spliced together from two different papers.
- Replaced a medRxiv preprint citation with the published *Sports Medicine* version
  (Ruijgt et al. 2026).
- Standardised the Hitch & Wrench handbook, which had appeared under five different
  spellings across the book.
- Added 216 DOI and PubMed links and 31 stable URLs. Two links that were about to be
  added turned out to be dead and were replaced before publication.
- Updated the RECOVER-AUTONOMIC ivabradine entry: that trial completed in December 2025.
